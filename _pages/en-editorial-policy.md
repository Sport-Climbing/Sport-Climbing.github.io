# Editorial policy and sources

## 1. Purpose and scope

Sport Climbing is a specialist reference. We produce, gather, examine and publish instructional, scientific and technical writing on sport climbing.

Passing information along is not the whole of the job. Readers, whether they climb, coach, do research or simply follow the sport, should be able to judge what we publish, trace it and use it with their eyes open.

The site is built around the three competition disciplines: bouldering, lead and speed. Training, physical preparation, the psychology of performance, biomechanics, equipment, competition rules and related research sit inside that frame.

News and events are part of the site. The editorial priority is still writing that is accurate, sourced and worth keeping, not the race to be first.

## 2. Editorial principles

What we publish should, as far as possible, meet the following:

* **Accuracy:** factual claims need sources that fit the claim and can be checked.
* **Transparency:** sources, the limits of what is known, and how sure a claim is should be stated plainly.
* **Editorial independence:** instructional and scientific writing should not be steered to a conclusion by a commercial interest.
* **Evidence kept apart from opinion:** research findings, personal experience, the author’s analysis and specialist advice are not interchangeable.
* **Kept up to date where it matters:** rules, competition results and equipment specifications change, and should be checked again when that change matters.
* **Responsibility:** on health and safety, we do not offer firm advice that the evidence will not carry.
* **Open to correction:** published work is not immune to error. People must be able to report a mistake, and we must be able to look at it and fix it.

## 3. How we rank sources

A source is only as good as the subject, the way the information was produced, the date and whether it can be verified. One ranking does not fit every subject.

### 3.1. Official and regulatory sources

For the competition disciplines, rules, scoring, event standards, rankings and official results, we give priority to:

* [World Climbing](https://www.worldclimbing.com/) and its official documents;
* the rules and official notices of a competition;
* documents from national federations and official organisers, where they are the right authority;
* official documents from bodies that set standards and equipment safety.

Where documents disagree, the version, the date it takes effect and the scope of each one have to be checked. A rule written for one event, or one jurisdiction, should not be treated as universal without saying so.

### 3.2. Scientific sources

For research and scientific writing, we prefer:

* research papers in reputable journals;
* systematic reviews and meta-analyses;
* recognised scientific guidelines and statements;
* relevant academic and specialist books;
* research reports whose methods are clear enough to be judged.

A DOI, an index entry or the fact of publication is not, by itself, proof that a claim is right. Study design, sample size, methodological limits, conflicts of interest and how far the results actually bear on the question all have to be weighed.

Where the full text is available, conclusions should be drawn from the full text, not from the title or the abstract alone.

### 3.3. Technical sources and manufacturers

On equipment, product specifications, instructions for use and technical limits, the manufacturer’s own documents and the standards that apply are what matter.

Advertising claims are not independent evidence that a product is better, or that it works as promised.

A comparison should make clear what is a stated specification, what comes from an independent test and what is user experience.

### 3.4. Experience and the climbing community

Ascent reports, coaches’ and athletes’ experience, and writing on forums and social media can be useful for knowing what happens in the field.

That kind of material can have practical value, especially where the conditions or the person make the difference. It does not automatically stand in for scientific research or an official instruction.

Experience should be presented as experience or as a point of view. It should not be turned into a general rule without enough evidence.

## 4. How we read a scientific study

Where a piece rests on scientific research, we try to look at the following before publishing:

1. **The question:** what, exactly, did the study examine?
2. **The method:** is the design, and the way things were measured, fit to answer that question?
3. **Who was studied:** which group of people do the results describe?
4. **Effect size and uncertainty:** has practical importance been confused with statistical significance?
5. **Limits:** which limits do the authors report, and which others may exist?
6. **How far it travels:** can the result be carried over to other disciplines, ability levels or climbing conditions?
7. **Conflicting evidence:** have other studies reported something different?
8. **Publication status:** has the paper been corrected, retracted or followed by a further notice?

Where the evidence conflicts, we do not pick out only the studies that suit a preferred conclusion. How far the evidence agrees, or does not, should be explained in proportion to what is actually known.

A statistical association between two variables does not, on its own, show that one causes the other.

## 5. Citations and transparency

Sources should be given so that a reader can identify them and, where possible, look at them.

A scientific citation includes the authors, the article title, the journal, the year and a DOI or a working link, as far as those details exist and can be confirmed.

For an official document, a link to the page or the document itself comes first.

A direct quotation should be visually distinct from the author’s explanation, and copyright has to be respected.

A translation, summary or rewrite should not change the original meaning or drop an important limit.

Citing a source is not an endorsement of everything in it. The source has to support the particular claim it is attached to.

## 6. Instruction and safety

Instruction should match the reader’s level, and it should not leave out prerequisites, limits or serious hazards.

Where the subject is the rope, belaying, falling, equipment, hard training or returning after an injury, the following apply:

* Important hazards, and situations in which the advice does not fit, are named.
* Anything said about using equipment is checked against the manufacturer’s instructions and the relevant standards.
* A partial set of instructions is not offered as a substitute for practical teaching and a qualified person watching.
* The limits of the information, and the need for a specialist assessment, are stated where they matter.
* Advice on treatment, diagnosis or rehabilitation is not stated as settled fact without a proper basis.

Publishing an instructional piece is not a guarantee that following it is safe in every setting. Safety in climbing also depends on skill, the environment, the state of the equipment and the decisions people make.

## 7. Independence and conflicts of interest

What we publish is chosen and judged for its instructional, scientific and practical value.

On products, services or brands:

* Technical specifications come from sources that can be checked.
* Personal experience is not presented as an independent test or a scientific result.
* A financial relationship, commercial support or a free product is disclosed when it bears on the piece.
* Payment does not buy a misleading claim, or the quiet omission of something important.

Sponsored or advertising material has to be distinguishable from independent editorial work.

## 8. Use of artificial intelligence

AI tools may be used to organise sources, draft, edit language or look at the structure of a piece.

Their output is not, by itself, a reliable source for a scientific claim, an official rule, an equipment specification or safety advice.

When these tools are used:

* Sources, quotations, links and anything that can be checked are checked.
* Invented citations and unverified claims are not published.
* The editors remain responsible for the accuracy and quality of what goes out.
* Where the use of AI has materially shaped a piece, and a reader needs to know that in order to understand it, that use is disclosed in a way that fits the piece.

AI does not replace human expertise, source-checking or editorial responsibility.

## 9. Corrections and updates

If, after publication, a piece turns out to contain a factual error, a wrong citation, outdated information or a misleading conclusion, we look at it.

Depending on the error, the response may be:

* correcting a typo or a slip in the writing;
* correcting the facts and the citations;
* adding a note on limits or on new evidence;
* revising the conclusion;
* publishing a correction notice where the change matters to the reader;
* removing or replacing something we can no longer defend publishing.

A substantial change should not be made in a way that hides, in an important case, that a correction was made or what it was.

Pieces that depend on rules, results, records, technical specifications or scientific evidence are reviewed again when they need to be. Where the date of the last review matters for judging how current the information is, that date is shown.

### Reporting an error

Readers can send possible errors, further sources or suggested corrections through the [contact page](/en/contact/).

A report is easier to look at if it includes the address of the page, the passage in question, a description of the error and, if possible, a source that supports the correction.

Receiving a report is not the same as accepting it. What is sent has to be checked.

## 10. Authors, expertise and review

What we say about authors, their expertise and other contributors has to be accurate and defensible.

An academic qualification, a competitive record, a coaching certificate or research experience is not attributed to someone without a proper basis.

Specialist review is mentioned only when it actually happened. If a review is credited, the reviewer’s name, field and kind of contribution are stated accurately.

Phrases such as “approved by experts” or “scientifically supported” are not used unless the evidence and the process match the claim.

## 11. Limits of responsibility, and independence from official bodies

Sport Climbing is an independent editorial project. Writing about World Climbing, official rules or sporting events does not mean that those bodies are affiliated with the site, that the site represents them, or that they have officially endorsed it.

On competition rules, the official documents are the authority for interpreting and setting the rules of an event. The site explains, teaches and analyses those documents. It does not replace the official text.

Instructional writing is not a substitute for practical teaching, a specialist assessment or the safety procedures a real situation requires.

## 12. Review of this policy

This policy may be revised as the site develops, as scientific and technical standards change, or as new needs become clear.

The current version of this page is the statement of the site’s editorial principles.

**The aim is simple: to publish information that can be checked, whose limits can be seen, and that can be used with a clearer head.**
